Crowded Room, Clear View

How one 3×3 matrix rescued a photo of Guernica

By Anabelle Brodsky and Hengchu Ye

Julia flew to Madrid to see Picasso's Guernica for her art history project, which is about why a painting of one bombed Basque town in 1937 became the world's best-known anti-war image. So did everyone else. Since the Museo Reina Sofía lifted its photo ban in 2023, there's a wall of phones in front of it.

The only gap Julia found was far off to the right. She got one photo, at a steep angle, with the far end of the painting fading away. She presents on Monday. Can math put the painting back?

Chapter 1

Four taps

A painting is flat, and a camera distorts anything flat in the same predictable way. For an ideal pinhole camera, one 3×3 matrix, called a homography H, describes that distortion exactly. (Real lenses add a little extra distortion that a homography can't model.) To find it, Julia only has to say where four points of the painting landed in her photo: its corners.

Each corner gives two equations. H has nine numbers, but scaling all of them changes nothing, so there are only eight unknowns. Four corners, eight equations, solved. Then for every pixel of a flat, straight-on canvas, the computer asks H where that spot was in her photo and copies the color.

The blue corners sit on the front corners of the canvas.

It's straight now, but something is still wrong. Everything looks squeezed from the sides.

Chapter 2

How wide is it, really?

To straighten the painting, Julia has to say what shape it should come out as. Her first guess was the shape of her phone screen, 4:3. That's why everything looks squeezed: the painting is much wider than that. Look at the bull's head and the light bulb at the top: they're pinched narrow.

Looking it up works for a famous painting. But the photo can answer by itself. In real life, the painting's top edge and side edge meet at a right angle. If we also assume a simple camera model (square pixels, no skew, no lens distortion, and the principal point at the center of the image), that one fact pins down the phone's focal length. Once the focal length is known, H reveals how wide the painting is compared to its height.

On Julia's real photo, the photo's own answer lands a few percent short of 2.22. The method assumes an ideal pinhole camera with its principal point exactly at the center of the image, and a real phone camera is only close to that. It's still close enough to fix the squashed look without looking anything up.

The math: here we use a version of the homography, G, that maps a unit square (0 to 1 on each side) onto the painting in the photo. With camera matrix K, K⁻¹G ∝ [a·r₁ b·r₂ t], where a and b are the painting's width and height, r₁ and r₂ are its axes as seen by the camera, and t is its corner's position relative to the camera. Because r₁ and r₂ are perpendicular, r₁·r₂ = 0 gives the focal length. Because both have length 1, the ratio of the first two column lengths gives a ÷ b.

Chapter 3

Where was she standing?

H also holds an estimate of where the camera was. Once the focal length and the painting's size are known, K⁻¹H ∝ [r₁ r₂ t] splits into the painting's axes as seen by the camera (r₁, r₂) and the painting's corner relative to the camera (t). With real, slightly imperfect taps those columns don't come out as a perfect rotation, so this gives a first guess. The page then fine-tunes the camera's rotation, position and focal length together until the painting's four corners land as close as possible to Julia's taps. The camera's position follows from C = −Rᵀt.

Chapter 4

What if she'd stood somewhere else?

Move Julia around the room with the W, A, S and D keys, or the buttons under the photo. W steps toward the wall, S backs away, and A and D move left and right. Her photo is rebuilt from each new spot, and the corners follow the painting.

Watch what changes and what doesn't. The corner pixels change with every step, so H changes too. The straightened painting doesn't, because undoing the angle is exactly what H is for. What does change is the detail: walk far to the left and the right side goes soft too, while the left side gets no sharper, because a rebuilt view can only use the detail in Julia's one photo.

The rebuilt view works because the wall is flat. Each new pixel is traced to a point on the wall with one homography, and that point is found in Julia's real photo with another. Two homographies chained together are still a homography.

A rebuilt view can only use what her one photo captured, so walking closer won't make the far side any sharper. The floor is drawn in, because it isn't on the wall.

Chapter 5

The visitor who wouldn't move

Julia went back for a second photo, and this time someone in a red coat stepped right in front of Guernica.

A homography describes one flat plane, and it assumes every pixel shows something on that plane. The visitor is standing 0.8 m in front of the wall, so H follows each ray from the camera through the visitor and keeps going until it hits the wall. The straightened version pastes the visitor onto the canvas as if Picasso had painted them.

And the paste is wrong in three ways. From where Julia took this photo, the visitor comes out about 1.4 times life size (about 2.4 m tall), about 1.6 m to the left of where they actually stand, with their feet about 65 cm below the floor. Nothing in the straightened image warns you.

The same thing happens to the white display case in the bottom-right corner of the photo. It's in front of the wall, so it turns into a pale wedge stretched along the bottom edge.

In symbols: u ∝ K[r₁ r₂ r₃ t][x y z 1]ᵀ. On the wall, z = 0 and the r₃ column drops out, leaving H ∝ K[r₁ r₂ t]. The visitor is at z = 0.8 m, so the r₃ term matters, and H can't know about it.

Chapter 6

Why the left side looks soft

Look closely at the straightened painting and its left side is blurrier than its right. From where Julia stood, the left edge was farther away and seen at a much more oblique angle, so it covers only a thin sliver of her photo. Straightening stretches that sliver back to full width, but it can't create detail the phone never captured.

Chapter 7

How far to the side is too far?

Next time, Julia wants to know how far to the side she can stand and still get a usable photo. Two things get worse as the angle grows: small mistakes in placing the corners get amplified, and the far edge gets less detail.

This test simulates 200 photos of Guernica at each angle from 0° (straight on) to 75°, in 5° steps. Each simulated phone matches Julia's: the same focal length and image size, at her distance from the painting's center (about 6.7 m). Each corner tap is off by a random amount, with a standard deviation of 2 pixels in each direction (about 2.5 pixels in total, on average). The results depend on these settings, so treat them as a guide rather than a rule.

Epilogue

Same math, different machines

Robots use this exact math all the time. A delivery robot turns its slanted camera view of the floor into a top-down map. A drone reads the four corners of an AprilTag to work out where it is. A phone's document scanner straightens a receipt.

Each one does what Julia did: four points on a flat surface, one 3×3 matrix. And each one has Julia's problems. Anything off the plane gets pasted in at the wrong size and place, like the visitor, and anything seen at a steep angle loses detail, like the left side of the painting. That's why robots that need to see obstacles add depth sensors or a second camera.
